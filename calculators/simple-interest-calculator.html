What is a Simple Interest Calculator?

Your cousin wants to borrow ₹50,000 for a year. He says "I'll pay 10% simple interest." You nod, but your brain freezes — how much is that at the end? Is it ₹5,000? ₹6,000? Just one number to know. Enter 50000 principal, 10% rate, 1 year. Result: ₹5,000 interest, ₹55,000 total. Done.

Simple Interest (SI) is the least confusing type of interest — it's calculated only on the original principal, never on accumulated interest. This calculator turns three numbers (principal, rate, time) into the interest amount and the total you'll pay or receive. Used in personal loans from friends and family, some car loans, and as a teaching tool for anyone learning how interest works.

How to Use This Simple Interest Calculator

Three fields. No modes. No confusion.

Principal: The original amount involved — the loan amount or the deposit. If you're lending ₹50,000, enter 50000. If you're depositing ₹1,00,000 in a scheme, enter 100000.

Rate: The annual interest rate. If someone says "10% per annum," enter 10. If they say "1% per month," enter 12 (since the calculator uses annual rate). This is where most users make mistakes — they enter the monthly rate directly. Always convert to annual.

Time: Duration in years. For 6 months, enter 0.5. For 18 months, enter 1.5. For 30 months, enter 2.5. Most users enter 6 for 6 months, which gives a result 6x too high. The calculator assumes years, not months.

The output shows two numbers: interest amount and total amount (principal + interest). Simple interest grows linearly — ₹1,00,000 at 10% for 5 years gives ₹50,000 interest, exactly 5 times the ₹10,000 earned in year 1. No compounding. That's the whole point.

The Simple Interest Formula (And Why It's Linear, Not Exponential)

The formula is: SI = (P × R × T) ÷ 100

Where P is principal, R is annual rate (%), and T is time in years. Total amount = P + SI.

Why is this different from compound interest? Because simple interest always calculates on the original P. In year 2, interest is still 10% of ₹1,00,000 — not 10% of ₹1,10,000. It doesn't grow. That's why simple interest is used for short-term loans and informal lending — the math is transparent and both sides can verify it easily.

Compare: ₹1,00,000 at 10% for 5 years, simple interest = ₹50,000. Compound interest (annual) = ₹61,051. The difference is ₹11,000 — that's the "interest on interest" that simple interest misses. Over long periods, the gap becomes enormous. Over 20 years, simple interest gives ₹2,00,000 and compound interest gives ₹5,72,750. That's why no bank offers long-term savings at simple interest.

Real Examples at Different Scales

Example 1 — Small Friend Loan (₹10,000 at 8%, 6 months)
Time in years: 0.5. SI = (10,000 × 8 × 0.5) ÷ 100 = ₹400. Total: ₹10,400. This is the classic "lend to a friend for 6 months" scenario. Simple interest is straightforward — the borrower returns ₹10,400 after 6 months. No compounding, no arguments.

Example 2 — Small Business Loan (₹2,00,000 at 12%, 2 years)
SI = (2,00,000 × 12 × 2) ÷ 100 = ₹48,000. Total: ₹2,48,000. A local shop takes a loan from an NBFC using flat rate. The interest is calculated on the original amount for the entire tenure. Compare with a reducing-balance loan at the same 12% — the actual interest would be roughly ₹25,000, half the amount. Flat-rate loans look cheaper on paper but cost more in reality.

Example 3 — Large Short-Term Loan (₹10,00,000 at 9%, 1 year)
SI = ₹90,000. Total: ₹10,90,000. A builder taking a bridge loan from a financier for 12 months. The financier doesn't compound — they quote a flat 9% per annum and the builder pays ₹10.90 lakh at the end of the year. Simple, no surprises.

Example 4 — Investment Return (₹5,00,000 at 7% for 3 years)
SI = ₹1,05,000. Total: ₹6,05,000. A bond that pays 7% simple interest annually. The bondholder gets ₹35,000 every year (or ₹1,05,000 at maturity). No compounding — this is what most fixed-income instruments offer on the surface. If the ₹35,000 yearly interest is reinvested, it becomes compound interest in practice, but the bond itself is simple interest.

Common Mistakes in Simple Interest Calculation

  • Using months instead of years. The formula requires T in years. For 6 months, use 0.5 — not 6. This is the single most common error and it makes the result 12x too high. If the calculator's output looks alarming, check the T input.
  • Confusing simple interest with flat-rate EMI loans. Flat-rate loans use simple interest on the full principal for the full tenure, then collect it via monthly EMIs. The displayed "flat rate" is roughly half of what an equivalent reducing-balance loan would charge. Always compare loans on reducing-balance terms, not flat-rate terms.
  • Forgetting tax on interest income. If you're receiving interest (on a loan given or a bond), it's taxable at your slab rate. ₹1 lakh interest received at 30% slab = ₹30,000 tax. Net in-hand is ₹70,000, not ₹1 lakh. Factor this before comparing to other investments.
  • Using simple interest for long-term investments. Simple interest is fine for 1-3 year horizons, but for 10-20 year investments, compound interest is dramatically better. Compare ₹1 lakh at 8% for 20 years: simple interest gives ₹2.6 lakh, compound gives ₹4.66 lakh. Never use simple interest for long-term wealth building.

When This Simple Interest Calculator Doesn't Apply

Simple interest has narrow use. It doesn't handle:

  • Compounding. Banks, mutual funds, and most savings schemes use compound interest. Simple interest is used only in specific informal or short-term scenarios.
  • Reducing balance loans. Home loans, car loans, and personal loans from banks use reducing-balance EMI calculations. The formula is different (the EMI formula).
  • Floating rates. If the rate changes during the loan period, you need to split the calculation into periods and add them up.
  • Part payments. If the borrower repays part of the principal mid-way, simple interest on the reduced principal is lower. This calculator assumes full principal throughout.
  • Day-count conventions. Banks use different day counts (30/360, actual/365) for interest calculation. The formula here assumes annual simplicity.

For bank loans, use the EMI calculator or compound interest calculator. Simple interest is best for informal lending or teaching purposes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is simple interest always better than compound interest?
For borrowers, yes — simple interest means paying less total interest. For investors, no — compound interest grows wealth faster. If you're borrowing, simple interest saves money. If you're investing, compound interest grows more. This is why banks offer compound interest on deposits but use reducing-balance EMIs (a variant of compound) on loans.

What's the difference between simple interest and flat rate?
Flat rate loans use simple interest on the full principal for the entire tenure, then collect it through monthly EMIs. A 10% flat rate loan is roughly equivalent to a 17-18% reducing-balance loan. Banks sometimes quote flat rates to make loans look cheaper — always convert to reducing-balance equivalent before comparing.

Can simple interest be compounded?
Not by definition. If interest is reinvested and starts earning its own interest, it becomes compound interest. Simple interest stays simple only if the interest is paid out (not reinvested) or the calculation is done once at maturity without reinvestment.

Is simple interest used in real banking?
Rarely. Banks use simple interest for a few retail products like some short-term loans from NBFCs (especially in rural areas) and post-office time deposits (some variants). Most bank products use compound interest. Simple interest is mostly used in informal lending, bonds (coupon payments), and educational contexts.

How do I convert simple interest to compound interest?
They're not directly convertible — different formulas, different results. If you want to compare, calculate both for the same principal, rate, and time, and see the difference. For short periods (under 1 year), the difference is negligible. For long periods (5+ years), compound interest gives significantly more.

Is interest on PPF simple or compound?
Compound — but with a twist. PPF interest is compounded annually, and the annual deposit compounds for the remaining tenure. So a 15-year PPF doesn't earn simple interest — it earns compound interest on a growing balance. This is why PPF maturity amounts are much higher than what simple interest would give.
