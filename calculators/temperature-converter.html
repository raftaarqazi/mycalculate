What is a Temperature Converter?

Your cousin is studying in Boston and calls to say "it's 68 degrees here today." You're in Chennai where the temperature is 32°C. Is 68°F hotter or colder than 32°C? You pause, do mental math — 68 minus 32 is 36, times 5/9 is 20. So it's 20°C in Boston, cooler than Chennai's 32°C. The mental math worked this time, but for values like -40 or 100, doing it in your head is error-prone.

This temperature converter does the math instantly. Enter any value in Celsius, Fahrenheit, or Kelvin, and it shows all three scales at once. Used by travelers checking foreign weather, students solving physics problems, cooks following American recipes, and anyone reading international news with temperature references.

How to Use This Temperature Converter

Two inputs. Three outputs. No ambiguity.

Temperature: The value you want to convert. For 32°C, enter 32. For -40°F, enter -40 (negative values are accepted — that's important, since temperatures below zero are common in many parts of the world). For 300K, enter 300.

From: The scale you're converting from. Select Celsius, Fahrenheit, or Kelvin from the dropdown.

The output shows all three scales simultaneously — Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin. Most users check only the one they wanted to convert to, but the all-three display saves a second lookup. If you entered Celsius, you immediately see the Kelvin value too, which is useful for scientific contexts.

One subtle but important detail: the calculator doesn't have a "To" field. It shows all three scales because temperature conversion is always a full set. If you know Celsius, you always get Fahrenheit and Kelvin for free. This is different from other converters (like length) where you might want km → miles but not km → feet. Temperature only has three scales in common use, so showing all three makes sense.

The Temperature Formulas (And Why They're Not Simple Multiplication)

Unlike length or weight conversions, temperature scales don't start at the same zero point. That's why the formulas have offsets.

Celsius to Fahrenheit: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

Fahrenheit to Celsius: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

Celsius to Kelvin: K = °C + 273.15

Kelvin to Celsius: °C = K − 273.15

The "+32" in the Celsius-to-Fahrenheit formula exists because 0°C equals 32°F — water freezes at different numerical values on the two scales. The "9/5" ratio exists because 100 Celsius degrees (from freezing to boiling) equal 180 Fahrenheit degrees. So each Celsius degree equals 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.

Kelvin has no negative values because 0 Kelvin is absolute zero — the theoretical point where all molecular motion stops. Nothing can be colder than 0K. That's why Kelvin is used in scientific contexts where negative temperatures don't make sense. On the Celsius scale, absolute zero is -273.15°C. On Fahrenheit, it's -459.67°F.

Here's why you can't just multiply: If you convert 20°C to Fahrenheit using the ratio 9/5 only (20 × 1.8 = 36), you get 36, which is wrong. The correct answer is 68. The missing 32 is the offset — the difference in where the scales start counting.

Common Mistakes in Temperature Conversion

  • Adding 32 before multiplying. The formula is (°C × 9/5) + 32, not °C × (9/5 + 32). Order of operations matters. 30°C is 86°F: 30 × 9/5 = 54 + 32 = 86 ✅. If you do 30 × (1.8 + 32) = 30 × 33.8 = 1014 — completely wrong. Always multiply first, then add.
  • Confusing negative temperatures. -40°C = -40°F — the only value where Celsius and Fahrenheit are equal. Users often think negative conversion works differently, but the formula applies the same way. -10°C = 14°F (not -50°F or similar).
  • Using 273 instead of 273.15 for Kelvin. The exact conversion uses 273.15 because absolute zero is -273.15°C. For everyday purposes, 273 works fine. For scientific work, use 273.15. The calculator uses 273.15 for accuracy.
  • Assuming temperature is a ratio scale. 40°C isn't "twice as hot" as 20°C in a meaningful physical sense. Temperature is an interval scale — differences are meaningful, but ratios are not. Users sometimes try to compare temperatures multiplicatively and get confused.

When This Temperature Converter Doesn't Apply

The three-scale conversion covers almost all everyday needs, but some edge cases exist:

  • Rankine scale. Used in some US engineering contexts (a Fahrenheit-based absolute scale). Not covered by this calculator. Rankine = Fahrenheit + 459.67.
  • Réaumur and Rømer scales. Historical scales still occasionally referenced in old European texts. Not covered here.
  • Wind chill and heat index. These are "feels-like" temperatures that factor in wind and humidity — not direct scale conversions. Different formulas.
  • Color temperature. Used for lighting (like 5000K for daylight). Same unit name (Kelvin) but measures a different physical property. Not a temperature in the thermodynamic sense.

For 99% of daily use — weather, cooking, fever, homework — the three scales cover everything.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Fahrenheit still used?
Historical reasons. The Fahrenheit scale was developed in 1724 and was widely adopted in English-speaking countries before Celsius became standard. The US, Bahamas, Cayman Islands, and a few other countries still use it for everyday weather and cooking. Scientific contexts use Celsius or Kelvin almost everywhere.

Is Kelvin better than Celsius for science?
Yes, for many calculations. Kelvin starts at absolute zero, so ratios and multiples work correctly — 300K is twice as hot as 150K in the physically meaningful sense. Celsius has an offset (0°C isn't "no temperature"), so comparisons are limited. Thermodynamics, gas laws, and quantum physics all use Kelvin.

At what temperature are Celsius and Fahrenheit equal?
-40 degrees. That's the only value where °C = °F. Below -40, Celsius is actually higher than Fahrenheit numerically. Above -40, Fahrenheit is higher. This crossover point is a useful anchor for sanity-checking conversions.

What is the normal human body temperature?
37°C or 98.6°F. But "normal" varies by person and time of day — 36.1-37.2°C (97-99°F) is the common range. A fever is generally defined as above 38°C (100.4°F). Different sources use slightly different thresholds.

Why is water's boiling point 100°C but 212°F?
Because the Celsius scale was designed with 0 as freezing and 100 as boiling — exactly 100 degrees apart. Fahrenheit used a different reference: 32 as freezing (based on brine) and 212 as boiling. The 180-degree difference between freezing and boiling in Fahrenheit is why each Celsius degree equals 1.8 Fahrenheit degrees.

Is 0°C the same as 0K?
No. 0°C is the freezing point of water (273.15K). 0K is absolute zero — the coldest possible temperature, where molecular motion theoretically stops. That's -273.15°C. A massive difference. Don't confuse the zeros.
